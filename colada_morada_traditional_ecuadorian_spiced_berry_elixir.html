Sacred Origins

An Ancestral Ritual for the Day of the Dead

Colada Morada is a cornerstone of Ecuadorian culinary identity, prepared exclusively around November 2nd for Día de los Difuntos (Day of the Dead). Its history bridges pre-Columbian Indigenous rituals with Spanish colonial influences.

Originally, native Quitu-Kara and Inca civilizations brewed a dark corn beverage infused with wild Andean fruits and llama blood as an offering to honor their deceased ancestors during the rain season. Over centuries, European aromatic spices like cinnamon, cloves, and sugar transformed the brew into the rich, sweet berry elixir enjoyed today.

"Every sip of Colada Morada carries the harvest of the Andes and connects the living with the memory of those who came before."

Culinary Process

How to Prepare Authentic Colada Morada

Preparing Colada Morada is a loving family affair. Takes roughly 2 to 3 hours of simmering and continuous stirring.

1

Steep the Aromatic Spice Infusion

In a large pot, simmer water with cinnamon sticks, cloves, allspice, sweet pepper, *ishpingo*, orange leaves, and lemongrass for 30 minutes until intensely fragrant. Strain and save the tea.

2

Prepare & Blend Fruit Purée

Cook the mortiño berries, blackberries, and naranjilla pulp with panela (unrefined cane sugar). Blend until smooth and strain out all seeds to create a concentrated berry base.

3

Dissolve Black Corn Flour & Combine

Dissolve black corn flour in cooled spiced tea. Whisk into the simmering pot along with the berry purée, stirring constantly over medium heat to prevent clumping until thickened.

4

Add Diced Fruits & Serve Warm

Gently fold in finely diced pineapple, babaco, and strawberries. Simmer for another 10-15 minutes until fruit tenderizes. Serve warm or chilled alongside fresh *Guaguas de Pan*.

The Inseparable Companion

Guaguas de Pan

Colada Morada is never served alone. It is traditionally accompanied by Guaguas de Pan ("Bread Babies" in Kichwa)—sweet, brioche-like decorative bread figures stuffed with chocolate, guava paste, or dulce de leche.

Historically, indigenous ancestors carved mummies or wooden figures to represent deceased loved ones. Today, these sweet breads decorated with colorful icing symbolize life, family renewal, and remembrance.

Sweet Brioche Guava / Chocolate Filled

Symbol of Life & Remembrance

Decorated intricately with royal icing in vibrant red, green, and blue patterns. Dunking a warm Guagua de Pan into cold or warm Colada Morada is the ultimate Ecuadorian culinary experience.
